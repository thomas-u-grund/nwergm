\chapter{Validation against ergm}\label{ch:validation}

\nwergm{} is a reimplementation, and the only reason to trust its numbers is that they agree with the program that it reimplements. This chapter describes how the comparison with \ergm{}~\ergmver{} was done, shows a comparison that was run for this manual, and lists the places where the two programs differ. The status of every term is in Chapter~\ref{ch:terms}. Appendix~\ref{app:quirks} lists the definitions of \ergm{} that \nwergm{} reproduces on purpose, and Appendix~\ref{app:samest} shows that the two programs run the same estimator and explains where the difference in speed comes from.

\section{How the comparison was done}\label{sec:howvalidated}

The reference is \ergm{}~\ergmver{} (R~4.6). The comparisons are of four kinds, from the strictest to the loosest. The first three are scripted and rerun by the test suite of the package, 66 do-files in the directory \texttt{cscripts}; the generators of the R reference values are in the directory \texttt{dev}.

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Observed statistics.} For every term, the observed statistic of test networks is computed by \nwergm{} and by \texttt{summary()} of \ergm{}. The test networks are random undirected, directed and two-mode networks with 14 to 100 nodes and the real networks of the statnet examples (the Florentine marriages, Sampson's monastery, the Kapferer tailor shop, Mesa High School). The statistics agree exactly, or to the floating-point precision of the statistic (at most $2\times10^{-7}$, for the term that \ergm{} computes in single precision).
  \item \textbf{Change statistics and pseudolikelihood.} The change statistics determine the pseudolikelihood. The change statistic of every dyad is compared with the difference of the statistic computed by brute force with and without the tie, and, for the two-mode terms, with the design matrix of \texttt{ergmMPLE()}. The pseudolikelihood coefficients and standard errors are compared with \texttt{ergm(estimate="MPLE")} on the same networks: the coefficients agree to $10^{-4}$ or better for every term that was compared, and to $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-10}$ for most (the column \emph{Validation} of Chapter~\ref{ch:terms} gives the figure per term), with the exceptions that Appendix~\ref{app:quirks} and Section~\ref{sec:differs} name. Where R's own fit is degenerate or separated (an infinite or missing coefficient, a standard error above 20), the comparison is not possible and the case is skipped.
  \item \textbf{Monte Carlo maximum likelihood.} For dyad-dependent models, the fit by \nwergm{} is repeated with several seeds and compared with several runs of \ergm{} on the same network. The criterion is that the difference of the mean coefficients is small compared with the standard error and with the run-to-run spread of both programs: for the models of the structural terms every coefficient was within 0.1 posterior standard errors of R's and inside the spread over seeds; for two-mode terms the differences of the means were between 0.01 and 0.14 standard errors. Constrained fits and fits with missing dyads were compared in the same way.
  \item \textbf{The sampler itself.} The Markov chains were checked against exact enumeration of the distribution on networks that are small enough (5-node undirected, 4-node directed and $3\times3$ two-mode networks; with every constraint that restricts the sample space) and against \texttt{simulate()} of \ergm{} (means and standard deviations of the statistics of long chains agree; Section~\ref{sec:cmpmanual} and Appendix~\ref{app:samest}).
\end{enumerate}
The estimation controls (the Hummel step length, the convergence test, the standard errors) were ported from the algorithms of \ergm{} and checked against intermediate results of R: the step-length routine against \ergm{}'s function to $10^{-7}$ (for models with more than 64 statistics the same linear program is solved by a revised simplex, which agrees with the original routine to $10^{-10}$), the multivariate spectral variance estimate used by \texttt{mcmc.diagnostics()} to $10^{-8}$, the effective sample size and the Geweke and Heidelberger--Welch statistics against \texttt{coda}.

\section{A comparison run for this manual}\label{sec:cmpmanual}

To show what these comparisons look like, the main ones were repeated for this manual on the networks of Chapter~\ref{ch:examples}. The Stata side is the do-file \texttt{manual/ex/ex91\_compare\_stata.do}, the R side \texttt{manual/ex/r/compare.R}; both read the same networks and attributes from files.

\paragraph{Observed statistics.} Table~\ref{tab:cmpstat} compares the observed statistics of the Lazega network. The statistics of the counts agree exactly; the geometrically weighted statistic, which is a sum of non-integer weights, agrees to \cmpStatMax.

\paragraph{Pseudolikelihood.} Table~\ref{tab:cmpmple} compares the pseudolikelihood estimates of four models: a dyad-independent model of the Florentine network, a dyad-dependent model (with \texttt{mutual}) of the Lazega network, and two models of the simulated two-mode network (one dyad-independent with \texttt{nodemix}, one dyad-dependent with two-mode degree and star terms). The largest difference between the coefficients is \cmpMpleMax{}; the standard errors agree to a relative difference of at most \cmpSeRel.

\paragraph{Monte Carlo maximum likelihood.} Table~\ref{tab:cmpmcmle} compares five runs of each program on the Lazega model of Section~\ref{sec:lazega}. With its default settings, which are those of \ergm{} (burn-in 16384, interval 1024, adaptive sample size, \ergm{}'s convergence test), \nwergm{} has a spread over seeds of the order of R's (standard deviations of the coefficients over the five seeds between 0.000 and 0.006 for both programs), and the means differ from R's by at most \cmpMcmleSd{} of a standard error; the standard errors of the two programs agree. The middle columns repeat the fits with a short chain and fixed-size samples (\opt{mcmleeffectivesize(0) mcmcburnin(3000) mcmcinterval(50)}): the chains are then short relative to the autocorrelation (Section~\ref{sec:lazega}), the spread over seeds is up to nine times larger (0.053 for \opt{edges}, against 0.006 with the default settings and 0.003 for \ergm{}), and single fits are off by a larger part of a standard error. Five seeds of one model are a small sample. The eight benchmark models of Appendix~\ref{app:samest} were also fitted five times each (seeds 1 to 5, default settings) by both programs: over the 27 coefficients the means differ by at most 0.09 of \ergm{}'s standard error, and the standard errors of \nwergm{} are 0.96 to 1.04 times those of \ergm{}.

\paragraph{The sampler.} Table~\ref{tab:cmpsim} compares the means and standard deviations of the five model statistics in chains simulated at the coefficients of the Lazega fit, by \nwergm{} and by \texttt{simulate()} of \ergm{}. The means differ by at most \cmpSimRel{} of a standard deviation and the standard deviations agree to the first digits.

\section{Where nwergm differs from ergm}\label{sec:differs}

The differences that matter to a user are these.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Monte Carlo sampling.} Burn-in 16384, interval 1024, a base sample of the larger of 256 and 32 times the number of coefficients, an effective-sample-size target of 64 and the convergence test with a tolerance region are R's defaults. Both programs stop after at most 60 iterations. The default proposal is \ergm{}'s SPDyad, and the effective sample size, the burn-in test and the convergence test are computed as in \ergm{} (Appendix~\ref{app:samest}). One difference remains: a cap of 32 times the starting values on the target and the base size, which \ergm{} does not have and which matters only in a fit that never converges. The pseudo-random streams differ.
  \item \textbf{Curved fits on boundary solutions.} Where the data do not identify the decay of a curved term, both programs end at a boundary, and the end points can differ: in a comparison with random networks, the fits of \texttt{gwespfree} (directed, OTP), \texttt{gwidegreefree}, \texttt{gwdspfree} and \texttt{gwnspfree} ended at a decay of about $10^{-12}$ in R and at another small value, $10^{-6}$ or less, in \nwergm{}. Where the data identify the curve the fits agree to $10^{-8}$ to $10^{-6}$.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{gwdegree} on a directed network.} R refuses it; \nwergm{} accepts it, with no counterpart in R to check the result against.
  \item \textbf{Controls not available.} \texttt{MCMLE.metric} (the log-normal approximation is the only one), \texttt{MCMLE.dampening}, \texttt{MCMLE.sequential}, \texttt{MCMLE.density.guard}, \texttt{parallel}, \texttt{monitor=}, \texttt{response=}, \texttt{esteq=}, and the constraint \texttt{constraints=}-arguments \texttt{ChangeStats()}, \texttt{Dyads()} and \texttt{I()}.
  \item \textbf{Proposals not available.} The Hamming proposals (reachable only through the broken \texttt{hamming} constraint), shared-partner proposals of other types than R's default (OTP on directed networks), \texttt{TNT10} (which R marks as flawed), the bipartite degree-distribution proposal (whose code writes the second toggle into the wrong slot), the degree-mixing proposal, and the stratified proposal of the \texttt{strat} hint. The bounded-degree proposal is realized as the tie/no-tie proposal with a rejection of moves that violate the bounds, which has the same target distribution.
  \item \textbf{Unbounded loops.} The degree-preserving proposals of R can loop forever on a state without a valid tetrad (for example on 4-node directed networks); \nwergm{} bounds the search at $10^{6}$ tries and treats the step as a null move.
  \item \textbf{Terms.} The arguments listed in the remarks of Chapter~\ref{ch:terms} that are not available, in particular \texttt{levels=} for the terms of \opt{structural()}; the form of \texttt{S()} with two sets of nodes; and the \texttt{Curve()} operator beyond linear maps.
  \item \textbf{Missing dyads and constraints together.} Missing dyads cannot be combined with \opt{freedyads()}, \opt{blockdiag()} or \opt{fixdensity}; an observation constraint in general is available through \opt{obsconstraints()}.
\end{itemize}

\section{How to reproduce}\label{sec:reproduce}

The comparison of Section~\ref{sec:cmpmanual} is rerun by \texttt{make compare} in the folder \texttt{manual} of the repository (Stata, and R with \ergm{}~\ergmver{}; about ten minutes): Stata writes the networks and its estimates to \texttt{manual/ex/r}, and the R scripts there read them and write their own. The tests of the package, with the reference values of \ergm{} built in, are run by \texttt{cscripts/run\_tests.sh}; the R scripts that generated the reference values are in \texttt{dev}. The benchmark of Appendix~\ref{app:samest} is in the replication files of the article on \nwergm{}.
