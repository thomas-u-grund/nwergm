\chapter{Limits}\label{ch:limits}

This chapter says what \nwergm{} cannot do, how large a network it can handle, and which problems are known. Numbers are measured and the way they were measured is given; where nothing was measured the text says so.

\section{Network size}\label{sec:size}

\paragraph{What costs what.} The pseudolikelihood is built over \emph{all} dyads, $n(n-1)/2$ in an undirected network and $n(n-1)$ in a directed one, so that time and memory of a pseudolikelihood fit grow with the square of the number of nodes, whatever the number of ties. The Monte Carlo sampler works on the ties and their neighbourhoods and its cost per proposal depends on the degrees, not on $n^2$, but every Monte Carlo fit starts from a pseudolikelihood fit (unless all coefficients are given in \opt{init()}) and so pays the cost of the dyad-level fit once, and the log-likelihood evaluation by bridge sampling needs further chains. A network is held in a sparse structure; dense $n\times n$ objects exist only for dyadic covariates (\opt{edgecov()}, \opt{hamming()}, \opt{dyadcov()}) and for \texttt{predict, matrix()}.

\paragraph{Measured run times.} Table~\ref{tab:scaling} gives the run time of \texttt{nwergm} on random undirected networks with mean degree 6 and three random groups, with the default settings and the native plugin, one fit per row (so the figures are indications, not benchmarks). The networks were generated by \texttt{manual/ex/ex98\_scaling.do} and \texttt{ex99\_scaling2.do}; the machine was an Apple-silicon Mac with 69~GB of memory running other jobs at the same time. The dyad-independent model has the terms \opt{edges}, \opt{nodematch(g)} and \opt{nodecov(x)}; the dyad-dependent model adds \opt{gwesp(0.5)} (Monte Carlo maximum likelihood with the default 3000 draws, with bridge sampling for the log-likelihood).

A random network has no clustering, so that the shared-partner statistics are small and the sampler is fast; a network with high degrees and heavy clustering is costlier per proposal and mixes more slowly, and the time of a Monte Carlo fit is dominated by the number of iterations and by the adaptive growth of the interval. These figures are \emph{not} a statement about such networks. Run times of \nwergm{} and \ergm{} depend on the model, the network, the settings and the load of the machine; two programs with different stopping rules and chain lengths are not compared by one example. \nwergm{} usually needs fewer iterations than \ergm{} on the models of this manual, with shorter chains per iteration; the price is a larger Monte Carlo error with default settings (Chapter~\ref{ch:validation}). No systematic benchmark against \ergm{} is part of this manual.

\paragraph{Larger networks.} Not measured: networks above 10,000 nodes, networks with mean degree above 6, models with many terms or with several dense dyadic covariates, memory use as a function of $n$, and fits of a directed network of the sizes in the table.

\paragraph{Stata limits.} Stata/BE has a maximum matrix dimension of 800 (\texttt{c(max\_matdim)}) and at most 2048 variables. A network with more than 800 nodes therefore cannot be declared from a Stata matrix with \texttt{nwset, mat()}; build it from an edge list (\texttt{nwfromedge}), as the networks of Table~\ref{tab:scaling} were. The same holds for \texttt{predict, matrix()}; \texttt{predict, frame()} has one row per dyad and is limited by memory. The matrix \texttt{e(mcmcsample)} has one row per draw; filling it from Mata is the slowest step for a very large \opt{mcmcsamplesize()} (more than 30 seconds at 100,000 rows in a test of the development of the package), and \opt{nomcmcsample} skips it. Other editions of Stata have larger limits; they were not tested.

\paragraph{Capacity of the plugin.} The plugin has fixed capacities: 256 coefficients (columns of the model), 64 distinct node attributes and 8 distinct dense dyadic covariate matrices in one model. A model that exceeds them is not refused; it runs in the Mata sampler as a whole, never partly in C and partly in Mata, and is much slower (and \opt{structural()}, \opt{operators()} and the constraints, which exist only in the plugin, are refused).

\section{The plugin and the platforms}\label{sec:platforms}

The native plugin is built for macOS (universal binary, compiled on the development machine), Linux and Windows (compiled by continuous integration). All the output of this manual comes from the macOS build with Stata/BE~18; the Linux and Windows plugins were not run for this manual, and the other editions of Stata (SE, MP) were not tested. The plugin reports its version, and a plugin that is older than the \texttt{.ado} files is detected. A platform without a plugin runs the Mata sampler, which is much slower for the models that have one (Table~\ref{tab:timing}) and does not exist for \opt{structural()}, \opt{operators()}, \opt{constraints()}, \opt{obsconstraints()} and \opt{mcmcmaxedges()}.

Two practical traps. A plugin in Stata's PLUS directory from an older installation shadows the plugin of a repository on the ado-path, so that a freshly built plugin can silently not be used; test it through a scratch ado-path. On macOS, overwriting a plugin that has already been loaded kills Stata without a message (the code signature is cached by the system); remove the old file first, then copy.

\section{Dialog boxes}

The three dialogs are generated from one specification and checked automatically: a script builds the command that the dialog would build for every term and option and runs it in Stata. They were not tested by hand on Windows or Linux, and the screen layout was not tested on other platforms than macOS. A dialog builds a single command line; model specifications that are easier to write by hand (long operator formulas, \opt{For()} loops) are better typed in a do-file.

\section{Known issues}\label{sec:issues}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Observed model statistics of \texttt{estat gof} after a two-mode fit.} The block \emph{model statistics} of \texttt{estat gof} shows the observed value 0 for the mode-specific terms of a two-mode model (for example the cells of \opt{nodemix()} or \opt{bgwdegree2()}), although the fit is right, and the Monte Carlo $p$-values of that block are then meaningless (the observed statistics of the same model in R's \texttt{summary()} are positive). The other blocks (the degree distributions of both modes, the shared partners and the distances) are correct. Use \opt{gof(\ldots{} -model)} for two-mode fits. Found while writing this manual.
  \item \textbf{Simulating a two-mode fit.} \texttt{nwergm simulate} from a two-mode fit with \opt{output(network)} creates one-mode undirected networks on all nodes, not two-mode networks; use \opt{output(stats)} or \opt{output(edgelist)}.
  \item \textbf{Large dyad counts in the pseudolikelihood start.} A fit with the shared-partner term on a random network of 5000 nodes (about 12.5 million dyads) stopped with r(198) \emph{observation number out of range}; the same model with 10,000 nodes ran. The cause is the conversion of the number of dyads to text in the fallback path of the pseudolikelihood step, which rounds it to three significant digits (\texttt{1.12e+07}) for some values; the number of dyads was not otherwise limited in the tests of Table~\ref{tab:scaling}. In the dyad-independent fit of 8000 nodes, which has 32 million dyads, the same step worked. No workaround is known. Found while writing this manual; not fixed in this release.
  \item \textbf{Degenerate and poorly mixing models.} A model that the data do not support makes the Markov chain degenerate. The fit then stops with the error of \ergm{} (Section~\ref{sec:kapferer}) or, worse, converges although the chain mixes poorly (Section~\ref{sec:lazega}). Once the chain does not mix, the thinning interval grows to its maximum of 20,000 proposals and every iteration costs a multiple of the first; the default 60 iterations then take many minutes. Lower \opt{mcmleiterations()} for a model that you suspect to be degenerate, and always look at \texttt{estat mcmcdiag}.
  \item \textbf{The Mata sampler can be extremely slow.} For the Lazega model it took 31 seconds against 0.6 seconds with the plugin (Table~\ref{tab:timing}); a fit of \opt{bgwdsp1(0.5)} on the 42-node two-mode network of Section~\ref{sec:twomode} with \opt{nonative} and \opt{mcmcsamplesize(500)} had not finished after 29 minutes of processor time and was stopped. \opt{nonative} is for testing and comparison.
  \item \textbf{Dyad-dependent constraints are slow.} A fit of \opt{edges mutual nodematch(prac)} to the 71-node Lazega network with \opt{constraints(bd(maxout=30))} took 165 seconds, against 0.16 seconds without the constraint (the bound does not bind: the largest out-degree is 22). Dyad-dependent constraints change the proposal and the starting values, and the time of such a fit is not predictable from that of the unconstrained model.
  \item \textbf{Standard errors of a pseudolikelihood fit of a dyad-dependent model} are not valid (Section~\ref{sec:mple}); \opt{mplecovariance(godambe)} or \opt{mplecovariance(bootstrap)} give covariances for the pseudolikelihood estimate.
  \item \textbf{No likelihood for a curved Monte Carlo fit.} \texttt{e(ll)}, AIC and BIC are missing after a fit with a free decay by Monte Carlo maximum likelihood (the pseudolikelihood fit has them), and \texttt{estat ic} refuses.
  \item \textbf{Missing dyads and constraints.} Missing dyads cannot be combined with \opt{freedyads()}, \opt{blockdiag()} or \opt{fixdensity}.
  \item \textbf{Collinear terms.} A set of terms that is exactly collinear (for example all levels of \opt{nodefactor()} with \opt{base(0)} together with \opt{edges}) is reported with a coefficient of 0 and no standard error; a statistic that never varies in the sample produces the warning that the model may be nonidentifiable.
  \item \textbf{Operators.} Operators cannot be combined with constraints, and \texttt{S()} and \texttt{Project()} do not accept dyadic covariates (Section~\ref{sec:operators}).
\end{itemize}
