\chapter{Limits}\label{ch:limits}

This chapter says what \nwergm{} cannot do, how large a network it can handle, and which problems are known. Numbers are measured and the way they were measured is given; where nothing was measured the text says so.

\section{Network size}\label{sec:size}

\paragraph{What costs what.} The pseudolikelihood is built over \emph{all} dyads, $n(n-1)/2$ in an undirected network and $n(n-1)$ in a directed one, so that time and memory of a pseudolikelihood fit grow with the square of the number of nodes, whatever the number of ties. The Monte Carlo sampler works on the ties and their neighborhoods and its cost per proposal depends on the degrees, not on $n^2$, but every Monte Carlo fit starts from a pseudolikelihood fit (unless all coefficients are given in \opt{init()}) and so pays the cost of the dyad-level fit once, and the log-likelihood evaluation by bridge sampling needs further chains. A network is held in a sparse structure; dense $n\times n$ objects exist only for dyadic covariates (\opt{edgecov()}, \opt{hamming()}, \opt{dyadcov()}) and for \texttt{predict, matrix()}.

\paragraph{Measured run times.}\label{sec:runtimes} Run times depend on the model, the network, the settings and the load of the machine. The default sampling of \nwergm{} is that of \ergm{} (Section~\ref{sec:adaptive}): the sample of an iteration has a few hundred draws, and the fit stops with \ergm{}'s test. Table~\ref{tab:bench} compares \ergm{}~\ergmver{} and \nwergm{}~1.5.2 on eight models, each with its default settings: median of seven fits (seeds 1 to 7) on identical networks, the programs run in turn on the same machine, one fit at a time, each fit in its own process after one untimed warm-up fit, and the time is that of the estimation call alone. The measurement was made on 9 November 2026 on an Apple-silicon Mac with 18 cores (the one-minute load average read before each fit was 1.8 to 3.4, median 2.6, about one of it the fit itself), with R~4.6.0 and Stata/BE~18; the times depend on the machine and are best read as ratios. The three undirected models were loaded as undirected networks for both programs. On these models \nwergm{} was 2.1 to 4.0 times faster than \ergm{}. The times of \ergm{} vary more between seeds than those of \nwergm{}, because the number of iterations varies, so the ratios are those of the medians. Both programs run the same algorithm with the same proposal: \nwergm{} makes about as many proposals in a fit (0.7 to 1.5 times as many as \ergm{}) and each proposal costs 2.2 to 4.6 times less, because the sampler runs in compiled code that keeps the statistics and the shared-partner counts of the current network up to date. The agreement of the estimates with \ergm{}'s is the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:validation}. Speed relative to \ergm{} depends on the model, and the table does not say that every model is faster.

A random network has no clustering, so that the shared-partner statistics are small and the sampler is fast; a network with high degrees and heavy clustering is costlier per proposal and mixes more slowly, and the time of a Monte Carlo fit is dominated by the number of iterations and by the adaptive growth of the interval. These figures are \emph{not} a statement about such networks. Run times of \nwergm{} and \ergm{} depend on the model, the network, the settings and the load of the machine; two programs with different stopping rules and chain lengths are not compared by one example, and the table of eight models above is the better guide. On the Lazega model of this table \nwergm{} took a median of 1.9 seconds (5 to 7 iterations) and \ergm{} 9.3 seconds (3 to 8 iterations), measured one after the other; the same model fitted with fixed 3000 draws took 1.8 seconds in one iteration for every seed. The Mata sampler (\opt{nonative}) is the slow fallback: 1,158 seconds (12 iterations) for one fit. The Monte Carlo error of the two programs is similar (Chapter~\ref{ch:validation}).

\paragraph{Postestimation and the other algorithms.} Single runs with 1.5.2 on the machine of Table~\ref{tab:bench} (9 November 2026, \texttt{manual/ex/ex9f\_posttime.do}; one run each, so the figures are indications): \texttt{estat gof, gof(model distance odegree idegree espartners)} after the Lazega model of Table~\ref{tab:bench} took 1.4 seconds and \texttt{nwergm simulate, nsim(100) output(stats)} 0.1 seconds; \texttt{estat gof} with its default statistics after \opt{edges nodematch(grade racen) gwesp(.25)} on Faux Mesa High took 1.1 seconds. On a random undirected network of 1000 nodes (mean degree 6) with \opt{edges gwesp(.5) nodefactor(g) nodecov(x)}, the fit by Monte Carlo maximum likelihood took 2.4 seconds, \texttt{estat gof} 26 seconds, \texttt{nwergm simulate, nsim(100) output(stats)} 0.15 seconds and the fit by stochastic approximation (\opt{mainmethod(sa)}) 16 seconds. These steps use the triad census by the algorithm of Batagelj and Mrvar, geodesic distances by breadth-first search over neighbour lists, observed statistics of large networks computed in the plugin, and chains that continue from the statistics of the previous draw instead of recomputing them.

\paragraph{Larger networks.} Not measured: networks above 10,000 nodes, networks with mean degree above 6, models with several dense dyadic covariates, memory use as a function of $n$, and fits of a directed network of the sizes in the table.

\paragraph{Models with one coefficient per node.}\label{sec:pernode} \opt{sender} and \opt{receiver}, and factor terms on an attribute with many levels, give a model with hundreds or thousands of coefficients. A dyad-independent model of this kind is fitted by the pseudolikelihood, which is then the likelihood, with a sparse design: \opt{edges sender receiver} on random directed networks took 0.3 seconds for 100 nodes (199 coefficients), 0.4 seconds for 200 nodes, 1.2 seconds for 400 nodes (799 coefficients; \ergm{} 573 seconds, estimates within $1.4\times10^{-6}$) and about 9 seconds for 1000 nodes (1999 coefficients, Stata/BE). A dyad-dependent model needs Monte Carlo maximum likelihood. As in \ergm{}, every iteration draws at least 32 draws per coefficient, so that the work of an iteration grows with the square of the number of coefficients: \opt{edges mutual sender receiver} on a random directed network of 100 nodes (200 coefficients) converged after 15 iterations in about 20 minutes, on a machine that also ran other jobs; \ergm{}, started on the same network on the same machine, had not finished its second iteration after an hour and was stopped. Such fits are practical up to a few hundred nodes. For larger networks, replace the per-node terms by node covariates or degree terms, or use the pseudolikelihood, which for a dyad-dependent model is only an approximation with too small standard errors.

\paragraph{Stata limits.} Stata/BE has a maximum matrix dimension of 800 (\texttt{c(max\_matdim)}) and at most 2048 variables. A network with more than 800 nodes therefore cannot be declared from a Stata matrix with \texttt{nwset, mat()}; build it from an edge list (\texttt{nwfromedge}), as the networks of Table~\ref{tab:scaling} were. The same holds for \texttt{predict, matrix()}; \texttt{predict, frame()} has one row per dyad and is limited by memory. Coefficient vectors wider than 800 (a model with one coefficient per node on more than about 400 nodes) are posted and read through Mata, so that \texttt{e(b)} and \texttt{e(V)} and the postestimation commands work. The matrix \texttt{e(mcmcsample)} has one row per draw; filling it from Mata is the slowest step for a very large \opt{mcmcsamplesize()} (more than 30 seconds at 100,000 rows in a test of the development of the package), and \opt{nomcmcsample} skips it. Other editions of Stata have larger limits; they were not tested.

\paragraph{Capacity of the plugin.} The plugin has fixed capacities: 8192 coefficients (columns of the model; a model with more than 256 receives its term table and returns its sample in frame variables), 64 distinct node attributes and 8 distinct dyadic covariate matrices in one model. The dyadic covariates are passed as their nonzero entries, so that their size is not limited by the number of variables of Stata/BE. A plugin binary of an earlier version of the package has a capacity of 256 coefficients and receives dense covariates; \nwergm{} checks the version of the plugin and adapts. A model that exceeds them is not refused; it runs in the Mata sampler as a whole, never partly in C and partly in Mata, and is much slower (and \opt{structural()}, \opt{operators()} and the constraints, which exist only in the plugin, are refused).

\section{The plugin and the platforms}\label{sec:platforms}

The native plugin is built for macOS (universal binary, compiled on the development machine), Linux and Windows (compiled by continuous integration). All the output of this manual comes from the macOS build with Stata/BE~18; the Linux and Windows plugins were not run for this manual, and the other editions of Stata (SE, MP) were not tested. The plugin reports its version, and a plugin that is older than the \texttt{.ado} files is detected. A platform without a plugin runs the Mata sampler, which is much slower for the models that have one (Table~\ref{tab:timing}) and does not exist for \opt{structural()}, \opt{operators()}, \opt{constraints()}, \opt{obsconstraints()} and \opt{mcmcmaxedges()}.

Two practical traps. A plugin in Stata's PLUS directory from an older installation shadows the plugin of a repository on the ado-path, so that a freshly built plugin can silently not be used; test it through a scratch ado-path. On macOS, overwriting a plugin that has already been loaded kills Stata without a message (the code signature is cached by the system); remove the old file first, then copy.

\paragraph{The source of the plugin.} The C source of the plugin is part of the package. \texttt{net install} installs the compiled plugins only; the command
\begin{lstlisting}
. net get nwergm, from(https://raw.githubusercontent.com/thomas-u-grund/nwergm/main)
\end{lstlisting}
copies the source files, the build rules and a short build note into the current directory. The same files are in the folder \texttt{native/} of the repository (\url{https://github.com/thomas-u-grund/nwergm}). \nwergm{} is free software under the GNU General Public License, version 3; the plugin interface files in \texttt{native/spi/} are StataCorp's Stata Plugin Interface, redistributed unchanged.

\paragraph{Building the plugin on your computer.} If the plugin of your platform is missing or does not work on your computer, compile it there with
\begin{lstlisting}
. nwinstall, buildplugin(nwergm)
\end{lstlisting}
The command copies the source from the repository, compiles it with the compiler of your computer (a C compiler and \texttt{make} are needed: on macOS the command line tools of Xcode, on Linux \texttt{gcc} and \texttt{make}), and installs the plugin next to the one that \texttt{net install} put into the PLUS directory; an existing file is kept with the extension \texttt{.prev}. Restart Stata afterwards. The plugin depends on the operating system and the processor, not on the version or the flavour of Stata. The routine was tested on macOS; on Windows it stops with a message and the build note that comes with the source gives the command for the Visual Studio command line.

\section{Dialog boxes}

The three dialogs are generated from one specification and checked automatically: a script builds the command that the dialog would build for every term and option and runs it in Stata. They were not tested by hand on Windows or Linux, and the screen layout was not tested on other platforms than macOS. A dialog builds a single command line; model specifications that are easier to write by hand (long operator formulas, \opt{For()} loops) are better typed in a do-file.

\section{Known issues}\label{sec:issues}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Valued networks are fitted as binary graphs.} A network flagged valued is fitted to the graph in which a tie is present when its value is not zero, with one note; the values are not an outcome of the model and there is no valued ERGM (Section~\ref{sec:data}, Section~\ref{sec:types}). Temporal networks are refused.
  \item \textbf{Reading results of other versions.} Results of fits made with other versions of \nwergm{} may differ; report the version printed by \texttt{nwergm version}.
  \item \textbf{Degenerate and poorly mixing models.} A model that the data do not support makes the Markov chain degenerate. The fit then stops with the error of \ergm{} (Section~\ref{sec:kapferer}) or, worse, converges although the chain mixes poorly (Section~\ref{sec:lazega}). Once the chain does not mix, the adaptive sample is extended and thinned in every iteration (at most 16 runs), a failed convergence test raises the target and the base size (up to 32 times their starting values), and every iteration costs a multiple of the first; the default 60 iterations then take many minutes. Lower \opt{mcmleiterations()} for a model that you suspect to be degenerate, and always look at \texttt{estat mcmcdiag}.
  \item \textbf{The Mata sampler can be extremely slow.} \nwergm{} prints one warning line before a Monte Carlo fit, \texttt{estat gof} or \texttt{nwergm simulate} that runs on the Mata sampler instead of the plugin, with the reason (the plugin file is missing for the platform, \opt{nonative}, or a model outside the capacity of the plugin); no warning is printed when the plugin is used. For the Lazega model with the default settings it took a median of 233 seconds against 1.1 seconds with the plugin (Table~\ref{tab:timing}); a fit of \opt{bgwdsp1(0.5)} on the 42-node two-mode network of Section~\ref{sec:twomode} with \opt{nonative} and \opt{mcmcsamplesize(500)} had not finished after 29 minutes of processor time and was stopped (a test during the development of the package, not repeated for this release). \opt{nonative} is for testing and comparison.
  \item \textbf{Dyad-dependent constraints are slow.} A fit of \opt{edges mutual nodematch(prac)} to the 71-node Lazega network with \opt{constraints(bd(maxout=30))} took 3.7 seconds with the default settings, against 0.3 seconds without the constraint (the bound does not bind: the largest out-degree is 22; both fits needed three iterations; \texttt{manual/ex/ex9d\_bdtime.do}). Dyad-dependent constraints change the proposal and the starting values, and the time of such a fit is not predictable from that of the unconstrained model.
  \item \textbf{Standard errors of a pseudolikelihood fit of a dyad-dependent model} are not valid (Section~\ref{sec:mple}); \opt{mplecovariance(godambe)} or \opt{mplecovariance(bootstrap)} give covariances for the pseudolikelihood estimate.
  \item \textbf{No likelihood for a curved Monte Carlo fit.} \texttt{e(ll)}, AIC and BIC are missing after a fit with a free decay by Monte Carlo maximum likelihood (the pseudolikelihood fit has them), and \texttt{estat ic} refuses.
  \item \textbf{Missing dyads and constraints.} Missing dyads cannot be combined with \opt{freedyads()}, \opt{blockdiag()} or \opt{fixdensity}.
  \item \textbf{Collinear terms.} A set of terms that is exactly collinear (for example all levels of \opt{nodefactor()} with \opt{base(0)} together with \opt{edges}) is reported with a coefficient of 0 and no standard error; a statistic that never varies in the sample produces the warning that the model may be nonidentifiable.
  \item \textbf{Operators.} Operators cannot be combined with constraints, and \texttt{S()} and \texttt{Project()} do not accept dyadic covariates (Section~\ref{sec:operators}).
\end{itemize}
